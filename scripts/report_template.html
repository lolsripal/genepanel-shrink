Can AI detect cancer using only a small gene signature?

We started from about 17,700 genes and asked how far a panel can shrink before cancer detection gets worse, and whether the genes that survive mean anything biologically.

Short answer. In tumor tissue, one or two well-chosen genes detect breast and lung cancer as accurately as all 17,700, including Stage I tumors. That is over 99.99% of the genes removed with no measurable loss. The reason is that tumor and normal tissue differ in hundreds of genes at once, so the signal is extremely redundant. Gene selection still matters: a random single gene reaches only AUC ≈ 0.70, versus ≈ 0.99 for a selected one.

5×5 repeated cross-validation, grouped by patient, with gene selection redone inside every training fold. Code, tables and notebooks: github.com/lolsripal/genepanel-shrink.

That is the main caveat for the whole study. Distinguishing tumor tissue from normal tissue is easy, because hundreds of genes each do it almost perfectly. So "small panels work" is expected. The informative comparisons are selected genes against random genes, and which genes get selected.

What the curves show

  • Selection matters only for small panels. With 1–5 genes, every supervised method beat all 20 random panels in every cancer and task. By 50 genes, random panels are within 0.002 AUC of the best.
  • Picking genes by variance fails. The single most variable gene does worse than a random one (AUC 0.46–0.68). In lung it is RPS4Y1, a Y-chromosome gene, with XIST next. Both track patient sex, not cancer.
  • Fewer genes can do better. In breast, selected panels of 10–500 genes have about half the error of the all-genes model (1 − AUC ≈ 3×10⁻⁴ vs 6×10⁻⁴). Thousands of irrelevant genes add noise.

Does the smallest panel retain performance?

A panel counts as retaining performance when the lower 95% bound of its paired, fold-by-fold AUC difference from the all-genes model stays above −0.01. The table gives the smallest panel size that passes, using the best selection method.

Model comparison

Best AUC across the five selection methods, per model. With enough genes the choice of model makes no difference. With one gene, tree models lose a little because they can only split a single value into steps.

XGBoost was also by far the slowest on all genes (about 19 s per fit, versus 1.5–3 s for the others) and gained nothing from the extra cost.

Which selection method picks the best genes?

Logistic regression AUC with 1, 2 and 10 genes. In lung, mutual information, RFE and SHAP find clearly better single genes than t-test ranking or LASSO (AUC 0.989–0.999 vs 0.974–0.984). In breast, every method is within 0.011 of the others, and for Stage I the t-test and LASSO come out slightly ahead. From 10 genes up the methods tie. No method wins everywhere, which is expected when hundreds of genes carry the same signal.

Stability

Different training folds pick different genes. At 10 genes, any two folds' panels share 35–80% of their genes, depending on method. Univariate ranking is the most stable, and RFE and SHAP the least. Accuracy is the same either way, because many genes are interchangeable. Treat any single published "signature" with that in mind.

Three independent checks

  • Literature. Selected genes have a median of 10 (breast) and 2–2.5 (lung) PubMed abstracts that also mention the cancer type, against 0–1 for random genes (one-sided Mann-Whitney p ≤ 0.013). MMP11 has 102 abstracts, NEK2 74, CA4 55, CAV1 49 and COL10A1 34.
  • Pathways. The top 100 univariate breast genes are enriched for the G2-M checkpoint and extracellular-matrix organization (Enrichr, FDR ≤ 0.001).
  • Cancer driver genes. At no panel size are the panels enriched for OncoKB cancer genes (hypergeometric p > 0.05). Drivers are often mutated without large changes in expression. A detection panel and a driver list answer different questions.

Cross-cancer transfer

The breast panel, retrained on lung data, detects lung cancer with AUC 0.998. A breast model applied to lung samples without retraining still reaches 0.997. Much of the signal is shared: tumors in different organs lose their normal tissue programs in similar ways.

How much biological information can we remove?

In tumor tissue, more than 99.99% of the measured genes, keeping one or two, with no measurable loss in detection. That holds for Stage I tumors and for all four models.

That does not mean cancer is simple. The detection signal is spread across hundreds of genes, so most of the transcriptome is redundant for this task and many different small panels work equally well. What small panels capture best is the loss of normal tissue architecture (stroma, fat, vessels, alveoli) rather than a cancer-specific program. That also explains why a breast panel works on lung tissue.

What this does not show, and what would come next

  • Tissue is not screening. Real early detection works on blood or other minimally invasive samples, where tumor RNA is heavily diluted. The next test is these panels on a liquid-biopsy (cfRNA) dataset.
  • Adjacent normal is not healthy. TCGA normals come from cancer patients. Adding healthy tissue from GTEx would test that.
  • One consortium, one platform. An independent GEO cohort or a qPCR assay would show whether 1–2-gene panels survive batch effects. They usually degrade faster than larger panels.
  • A harder task would separate the methods. With AUC ≈ 0.999 everywhere, differences between methods are tiny. Subtype or stage prediction would discriminate between them better.

Methods: label-free gene filter (expressed above log₂(RSEM+1) = 1 in ≥ 20% of samples, SD ≥ 0.1). LASSO, MI, RFE and SHAP rank genes within the top 2,000 univariate genes of each training fold. Logistic regression C is tuned by inner 3-fold CV. RF uses 300 trees, XGBoost 300 rounds at depth 3, and the MLP has 64-32 hidden units with early stopping, all with class balancing. Confidence intervals treat the 25 repeated-CV folds as independent, so they are somewhat too narrow.
