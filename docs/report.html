How small can a cancer test get before it stops working?

I took gene-expression data from tumors, healthy tissue and blood, and kept shrinking the set of genes a model was allowed to use, from down to one, to find where cancer detection breaks.

Short answer: it depends on where you look, and the easy cases are misleading.

  • In tumor tissue, one or two genes are enough (AUC ), because tumor and normal tissue differ in hundreds of genes at once.
  • In blood, a real early-detection setting, small panels lose accuracy: 1 gene reaches AUC , 10 genes , all genes . And part of the signal comes from which hospital the blood came from and how old the patients were, not from cancer.
  • On new data, tiny panels are the first to break. A 1-gene lung test that rejected of normal samples in its own dataset rejected only of healthy lungs from another pipeline.

Hundreds of genes each separate tumor from normal tissue almost perfectly, so this result says more about the task than about the methods. The genes that matter are mostly normal-tissue genes that tumors lose (fat, vessels, alveoli), plus stroma-remodelling genes such as COL10A1 and MMP11. The next three chapters test whether that optimism survives harder conditions.

Chapter 2A blood test: where panel size starts to matter

Tumor-educated platelets are blood cells that pick up RNA changes when a tumor is present. I reanalyzed two public studies: 779 people tested for lung cancer (GSE89843), and 285 people with one of six cancers or none (GSE68086).

Unlike tissue, no small panel matched the full model in any blood task. Accuracy climbs steadily from 1 to 100 genes and beyond. Gene selection still matters a lot: the best 10 genes beat 10 random genes by a wide margin.

Is the model detecting cancer, or the hospital?

In this dataset, only one hospital (VUMC) sent both cancer and non-cancer samples. Every other hospital sent only one kind: cancers from MGH and NKI, controls from UMCU, AMC and UMEA. Cancer patients were also about 17 years older. A model that sees only hospital, age, sex and smoking, and no genes at all, reaches AUC .

Four checks show how much real signal is left:

  • Site fingerprint. Among people without cancer, platelet RNA tells VUMC samples from other hospitals' samples with AUC . So a hospital signature is in the RNA itself.
  • One hospital only. Within VUMC, where hospital cannot help, all genes still reach AUC .
  • Matched cohort. In the authors' age/sex/smoking-matched subset, metadata alone drops to AUC , while genes keep (10 genes: ). This is the cleanest evidence of a real cancer signal.
  • Early cancer. For non-metastatic cancer, age + sex + smoking alone reach AUC , about as high as the gene model (). With only 57 such patients, this task cannot show a cancer-specific signal.

Chapter 3Healthy controls and new data

TCGA's "normal" samples come from cancer patients. To compare tumors with truly healthy tissue, I used the UCSC Toil dataset, which runs TCGA and GTEx (tissue from people without cancer) through the same RNA-seq pipeline.

A warning from the controls. TCGA adjacent-normal tissue vs GTEx healthy tissue, where both groups are "normal", can be told apart with AUC using just 2 genes; even one random gene gives . The two studies differ so much (in collection, surgery and processing) that "tumor vs GTEx" cannot be read as tumor vs healthy. Any model trained on it partly learns which study a sample came from.

A cleaner test: freeze the Chapter 1 models, which were trained on TCGA tumors vs adjacent normals in the original pipeline, and apply them unchanged to tumors from other patients plus GTEx healthy tissue in the Toil pipeline. Each gene is matched to the training scale using samples that exist in both pipelines, from training patients only.

Chapter 4Harder questions: telling cancers apart

Detection asks "cancer or not?". These tasks ask "which kind?". They show where the panel-size curve really bends.

  • Lung adenocarcinoma vs squamous carcinoma: 1 gene reaches AUC , 10 genes , all genes (10 random genes: ). Smallest panel matching all genes: .
  • Breast cancer PAM50 subtypes (5 classes): balanced accuracy with 1 gene, with 10, with all genes. Smallest panel matching all genes on AUC: .
  • Which of 12 cancer types a tumor is: balanced accuracy with 1 gene, with 10, with all genes (chance: 8%). Smallest panel matching all genes on AUC: .

What sets the panel size is how distinct the groups are, not how many there are. Twelve tissues of origin each have their own lineage genes (TG for thyroid, NKX3-1 for prostate, GFAP for brain, GATA3 for breast and bladder), so about 10 genes, roughly one per tissue, are enough. Five breast subtypes shade into each other along estrogen-receptor, HER2 and proliferation programs, so they need about 50 genes. The selected genes are textbook markers: ERBB2 (HER2), ESR1, FOXA1 and FOXC1 for breast subtypes, and the squamous keratins KRT5 and DSG3 for lung. A single gene can draw one boundary, so it cannot name 12 cancer types.

Does the model matter?

Barely. Across tasks, logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost and a small neural network land close together. Simple logistic regression is best with all genes in 15 of the 19 tasks, and where another model wins, it is by at most 0.003 AUC. The other models struggle more with thousands of noisy inputs.

Which genes get picked?

The genes selected most often across training folds and methods (consensus panels). In tissue they mark lost normal tissue and tumor stroma, and for cancer types they are well-known lineage and subtype markers. In the six-cancer blood study they are T-cell genes (CD27 IL7R TRBC2), which hints at immune or white-cell differences rather than platelet changes. For lung cancer they include platelet and cytoskeleton genes (CFL1 ITGB3 TIMP1). After correcting for the hundreds of panels tested, tissue and blood panels are not enriched for known cancer driver genes (OncoKB): detection relies on large, consistent expression changes, and most drivers are not that kind of gene.

What it means

How much biological information can we remove? In tumor tissue, almost all of it, but only because the task is easy. In blood, the realistic setting for early detection, every gene removed below about 100 costs accuracy, and small panels also break first when the data change.

  • Small panels are not free. They match big models only when the signal is huge and redundant. When the signal is weak, or the data come from a new hospital or pipeline, bigger panels are more accurate and more robust.
  • Check the confounders first. Age, hospital and study origin explain a large share of several headline AUCs here. Checks like a metadata-only model and a matched cohort should come before any claim about a biomarker.
  • A threshold is part of the test. AUC can stay high while the cutoff chosen on one dataset misclassifies healthy people in another.

Limitations

  • All data are public and retrospective. The blood cohorts are small, especially for early-stage cancer (57 non-metastatic patients).
  • Hospital and age are entangled with cancer status in GSE89843. The matched cohort reduces this but is smaller.
  • TCGA vs GTEx differences are partly technical, so Chapter 3 cannot fully separate "healthy" from "different study".
  • Cross-validation intervals use a corrected test (Nadeau–Bengio), but folds still come from one population per dataset.

Methods

  • Validation. 5-fold cross-validation repeated 5 times (2 for the 12-type task), grouped by patient. Gene selection is redone inside every training fold, so test samples never influence which genes are chosen. A unit test checks this.
  • "Matches all genes". The lower 95% bound of the paired, fold-by-fold AUC difference stays above −0.01, using the Nadeau–Bengio correction for overlapping training sets.
  • Data processing. Tissue data are log2 RSEM (TCGA via UCSC Xena) or log2 TPM (Toil). Platelet data are raw counts converted to log2 counts per million. Genes not expressed in at least 20% of samples are dropped (50% for GSE68086).
  • Models. Logistic regression (regularization tuned by inner CV), random forest (300 trees), XGBoost (300 rounds, depth 3), and a neural network with two hidden layers (64, 32). All are class-balanced.
  • Confounding checks. Models on metadata alone, a site test on non-cancer samples, one-hospital and matched-cohort reruns, an age-restricted re-scoring, and the authors' own train/validation split.
  • Calibration and decision curves. Computed from out-of-fold predictions, with each sample predicted once.

How this was built

Python (scikit-learn, XGBoost, SHAP, pandas): about 2,100 lines of code, 35 automated tests, and continuous integration on GitHub. All results regenerate from public data with one command per chapter. Every number on this page is filled in from the result tables when the page is built.

Data: The Cancer Genome Atlas and GTEx via UCSC Xena and the UCSC Toil recompute; platelet RNA-seq from GEO (GSE89843, GSE68086; Best et al., Cancer Cell 2017 and 2015). Code and every table: github.com/lolsripal/genepanel-shrink.
